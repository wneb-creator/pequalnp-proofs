# Necessary conditions on missing rows and branching inputs

This note records a mathematical derivation and a finite diagnostic census. The statements have not been checked in Lean. They use the connected single-point carrier and the missing-row dominator argument in the sibling restriction note.

Work in a strict, live binary circuit for a finite rectangle-free bipartite graph whose two shores have sizes $k,b$. Assume every variable has degree at least two, so distinctions within either shore have incomparable neighborhoods. Let $B$ be the sum of the excess outdegrees. A point-centered improvement needs $B$ at most the number of edges minus $b+1$.

Starting at an input, follow its unique reader until the first branching wire or the output. A wire on that initial path dominates the input. If it accepts a singleton of a different variable, there is an independent input containing that other variable and a neighbor of the dominated variable. Indeed, on the same shore use incomparability of neighborhoods, and on opposite shores choose a neighbor in the other variable's shore. Adding the dominated variable to that independent input must change the output but leaves the already true dominating wire fixed. This contradiction shows the only possible singleton at a dominating wire is its own variable. An input is eligible when its first branch accepts its own singleton. Its first branch then reserves a surplus unit carrying exactly that variable. Along its initial path every wire up to that branch also accepts this singleton and no other, so the reserved branch belongs to the root-connected pure carrier.

A baseline neighborhood OR accepting at least two point singletons implies that each of its point labels is eligible. Otherwise the neighborhood wire would either be on the input's unique initial path (contradicting acceptance of a second singleton) or downstream of a dominating branch false on that singleton (making it agree with the all-zero input). The same holds on the other shore.

For each missing line the dominator argument supplies a distinct first branch with empty point-singleton support. If the line is ineligible then its first branch accepts no singleton of either color. There is an analogous distinct set for missing ineligible points. The two empty sets of reserved wires for the two colors may overlap; their union costs at least the larger cardinality. These empty branches are disjoint from every eligible singleton reserve.

If a missing line is ineligible, its first branch rejects the line singleton. Thus there is an AND on its unique prefix, before any branch. The other operand must accept the singleton of every neighboring point, since setting that point and the line makes the output true and the line must affect the output through this gate. This shows the neighbors are eligible. For a present line, all neighbors are eligible by its baseline OR. Every edge consequently has an eligible endpoint.

For fixed eligibility and row presence statuses, let $r_i$ count the incident present lines at center $i$, and let $u_i$ indicate whether any incident line is missing. Its pure carrier contributes at least the larger of its eligible flag and $r_i+u_i-1$ surplus units. The carriers for different points are disjoint, as are all line singleton reserves and singleton-empty first branches. Apply the analogous line-carrier sum as a second necessary bound.

There is a further bound for the full carrier of a point $i$, consisting of all wires accepting singleton $i$. Its surplus is at least its exit count minus one by arc counting. Each incident ineligible line $l$ has a first AND on its unique prefix. The incoming line arm at this AND accepts only the line singleton, and the other arm accepts every point singleton in its neighborhood. Thus at this gate there is an exit from the full carrier of $i$. Such gates for distinct ineligible lines are distinct: their line arms cannot have two singleton labels, and the opposite arm accepts at least two point singletons. If an eligible line $a$ is also incident to $i$, use the positive flag $(i,a)$ and the maximal negative consisting of $a$ plus all points outside its neighborhood. The output has opposite values and only point $i$ is positive-only, so following a positive child at ORs and a negative child at ANDs terminates at $i$. Any exit on the reversed path is different from the exits just counted. At a counted first AND for $l$, the carrier-side operand is true on this negative because it accepts another point on $l$ outside $a$. The path would therefore follow the line-side operand, which is outside the carrier. Hence the full carrier requires at least the number of incident ineligible lines plus an indicator for an incident eligible line minus one units.

To upper-bound these available units at $i$, first reserve the distinct eligible units of both colors and the required singleton-empty units. Let $R$ be the surplus cap minus those reservations. Pure-carrier requirements beyond an eligible point's reservation consume disjoint units. Those mandatory extra units of other points cannot be in $i$'s full carrier. Thus the full carrier of $i$ contains at most its own eligibility flag plus $R$ minus all these mandatory extra units of other points. Apply the same calculation on the other shore.

The two Python census programs enumerate all eligibility sets satisfying the cover and all possible present subsets with eligible neighbors. They test the displayed inequalities. For all noncollinear PG(2,3) cores with at most five selected points no status survives one below the point-star bound. For six points, types 0 and 3 have no survivors, type 1 has 64 and type 2 has three. Type 1's secants equal those of the Fano plane with a point removed in the listed coordinates; a one-gate saving would extend by a four-gate point star to 26 gates for Fano. Type 2's three examples use all eleven allowed surplus units as distinct singly charged eligible first branches. The reported inconsistent statuses are arithmetic diagnostics conditional on the mathematical inequalities.
